The framework at a glance

The 32 competencies, across six domains. Use the Ref numbers (like 4.1) when you answer questions later.

U = Universal, for every graduate    C = Contextual, depends on your discipline    A/H = Arts and Humanities
Domain 1: Conceptual Understanding of AI Systems
1.1Understanding How AI Systems Are Built and Trained by DataAI learns patterns from huge amounts of data rather than being given rules, so what goes into the data shapes what comes out.U
1.2Understanding Different AI Model Types and Their ApplicationsThere are different kinds of AI (text, image, prediction) and each is suited to different jobs.U
1.3Identifying Fundamental Limitations and Failure Modes of AI SystemsKnowing what AI genuinely cannot do, and the typical ways it goes wrong.U
1.4Recognising How AI Shapes Everyday Services and DecisionsSpotting where AI is quietly at work in things you use daily, like recommendations, banking or job applications.U
Domain 2: Critical Evaluation of AI Outputs and System Performance
2.1Evaluating the Accuracy and Reliability of AI-Generated InformationChecking whether what AI tells you is actually true, instead of taking it at face value.U
2.2Detecting Bias and Unequal Representation in AI OutputsNoticing when AI answers favour some groups of people or leave others out.U
2.3Analysing the Logical Consistency of AI-Generated ReasoningFollowing the AI's reasoning to see whether the steps actually hold together.U
2.4Assessing AI Outputs for Professional Tone and Contextual FitJudging whether the style and tone of an AI answer suits the situation you would use it in.U
2.5Verifying the Authenticity of AI-Generated Synthetic MediaWorking out whether an image, video or voice is real or AI-made, such as deepfakes.U
2.6Evaluating the Credibility of Information and Claims About AITelling solid evidence about AI apart from hype and marketing claims.U
Domain 3: Ethical and Social Implications of AI
3.1Evaluating Personal Data Footprints Within AI SystemsUnderstanding what personal data you hand over when you use AI, and where it ends up.U
3.2Assessing the Environmental Impact and Sustainability of AI TechnologiesAI uses real energy and water, so knowing the environmental cost of using it.U
3.3Examining AI’s Broader Impact on Work and SocietyThinking about how AI is changing jobs, industries and communities, including your own future field.U
3.4Preserving Human Agency and Decision-Making Authority in AI-Assisted ProcessesMaking sure people, not systems, stay in charge of decisions that matter.U
3.5Analysing AI’s Impact on Information Integrity and Public DiscourseUnderstanding how AI affects what we can trust in news and public debate.U
3.6Recognising the Psycho-Emotional Effect of Human-Like AI Responses on Human UsersNoticing how it feels when AI sounds human, and how that can affect trust or attachment.C
3.7Identifying Professional Contexts Where AI Use Is InappropriateKnowing the situations in your field where using AI would be wrong or harmful.U
Domain 4: Practical Application and Professional Use of AI Tools
4.1Designing Effective Prompts and Queries for AI SystemsAsking AI good questions so that you get genuinely useful answers.U
4.2Selecting and Integrating AI Tools in Professional WorkflowsChoosing the right tool for a task and fitting it sensibly into how you work.U
4.3Using AI as a Tool Whilst Preserving Critical JudgementLetting AI help you without handing over your own thinking.U
4.4Diagnosing and Resolving AI Failures and Unhelpful Outputs in PracticeWorking out why AI gave you something useless, and fixing your approach.U
Domain 5: Governance, Accountability, and Regulation of AI
5.1Examining AI Tools for Trustworthiness, Transparency, and ReliabilityAsking whether a tool is open about how it works and whether it can be relied on.U
5.2Disclosing and Documenting AI Use and Contributions in Academic and Professional WorkBeing open about where and how you used AI in a piece of work.U
5.3Interpreting Relevant Legal and Regulatory Frameworks Governing AIKnowing the basic laws and rules that apply to AI in your area.U
5.4Applying Professional Ethics and Sector-Specific Codes to AI UseApplying your profession's own code of conduct to the way you use AI.U
5.5Evaluating Intellectual Property, Copyright, and Training Data Ethics in AIUnderstanding who owns what, when AI is trained on other people's work and when it creates something new.U
5.6Reflecting on Personal and Professional Accountability in AI-Assisted WorkRecognising that you stay responsible for work you produced with AI's help.C
5.7Understanding AI Governance and Regulatory Responsibilities in Professional PracticeKnowing who oversees AI use in a workplace, and what your own duties are.C
Domain 6: Cognitive Diversity, Accessibility, and Inclusive AI Literacy
6.1Inclusive and Adaptive AI UseUsing AI in ways that work for different needs, including as assistive technology.U
6.3Evaluating AI Tools for Equity and Inclusive AccessJudging whether a tool is usable and affordable for everyone, not just for some.U
6.4Reflecting on AI’s Impact on Personal Learning and Cognitive DevelopmentThinking about whether using AI is helping you learn, or doing the learning for you.U

Section 3: Reacting to assessment

Universities also have to decide how to assess these skills. Here are three real example approaches.

Example A: AI interaction transcript analysis

You complete a task using an AI tool, then submit the full transcript with a commentary explaining where the AI helped, where it went wrong, and what you changed.

Example B: Oral “defend your AI use” (viva)

A short spoken conversation where you explain and justify how you used (or chose not to use) AI in a piece of work.

Example C: Human vs AI parallel task

You do part of a task yourself and have AI attempt the same part, then compare and critique the two.

Section 5: Fair and accessible?

Could every student develop these fairly? The key test is whether something is a structural barrier, not whether a determined student could work around it.

Equity dimensionThe question to ask yourself
Access and resourcesDoes developing this assume paid AI tools, reliable broadband, or specific devices that not all students have?
Prior knowledge and experienceDoes it assume computing or technical background that students from under-resourced schools or non-technical subjects may not have?
Language and communicationIs the language accessible to students with English as an additional language, or who have dyslexia or other processing differences?
Neurodiversity and learning stylesAre there multiple ways to demonstrate it, or does it privilege one mode (e.g. written argument) that disadvantages neurodivergent students?
Cultural and contextual assumptionsDoes it reflect mainly Western or Anglophone norms about AI, ethics, or professional practice?
Structural and systemic barriersDoes it recognise that AI itself can embed discrimination, and that some groups are more likely to be harmed than helped?
